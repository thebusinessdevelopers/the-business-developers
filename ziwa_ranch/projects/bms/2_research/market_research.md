# BMS Research — Market Research

> **Summary:** The market for BMS is lodges, hotels, and tourism operations in Uganda and East Africa — organisations that would buy the software. Uganda's tourism sector is growing fast, under-digitised, and operating largely on paper and WhatsApp. The technology purchasing gap is wide. Infrastructure constraints (intermittent internet, mobile money dominance, power unreliability) are real and must shape the product. The addressable market in Uganda alone is hundreds of properties, with Kenya and the broader East Africa region extending that significantly.

---

## What Market We Researched and Why

BMS is a hybrid product — Joshua uses it himself at Ziwa, but the intent is to sell it to other lodges and hotels. The relevant market is therefore the **organisations that would buy BMS**: hospitality businesses in Uganda and East Africa that need better operational management. We focused on Uganda as the primary geography (Ziwa is there, Joshua knows the ground), with East Africa context where the data was stronger or directly applicable.

## 1. The East African and Ugandan Hospitality Sector

### Scale

Uganda's tourism sector is substantial and growing:

- **1.37 million international arrivals in 2024** — 7.7% growth year-on-year, reaching 89.2% of pre-pandemic levels. (Source: Uganda Ministry of Finance Development Policy and Performance Portal, Tourism Trends Report 2024.)
- **USD 1.28 billion in tourism earnings in 2024** — up 25.9% from 2023. (Source: Red Pepper Business, sourcing Uganda Tourism Board data.)
- **Tourism contributes 12.8% of Uganda's GDP** — approximately $10.5 billion including indirect effects, with 22.3% growth year-on-year. Foreign exchange earnings from tourism reached $2.5 billion. (Source: Uganda E-Visa tourism economic analysis, 2024 — note: these figures are from a third-party analysis site, treat with some caution.)
- **803,000 jobs** in the tourism sector as of 2024.
- Uganda expects **1.6 million arrivals by 2025**, continuing the upward trend.

### The accommodation landscape

Uganda Tourism Board data shows the formally graded accommodation sector:
- 4 five-star facilities
- 17 four-star facilities
- 23 three-star facilities
- 59 two-star facilities
- **103 graded facilities total** (as of November 2025, UTB graded facility list)

This is the tip of the iceberg. The Uganda Hotel Owners' Association (UHOA) has over **780 member hotels**, and the full number of licensed accommodation establishments (budget lodges, guesthouses, camps, tented camps) substantially exceeds this. Uganda's national parks alone — Bwindi, Queen Elizabeth, Murchison Falls, Kibale — host dozens of lodge and camp operations not captured in the graded five-star-to-two-star structure.

**East Africa context:** Kenya and Tanzania have significantly larger hotel pipelines. Kenya and Ethiopia lead the region with 79.5% and 79.9% of their hotel pipeline rooms under construction respectively, with global brands (Marriott, Hilton, Accor) expanding across the region. (Source: CEO East Africa, citing regional hospitality pipeline data.) This signals growing demand for sophisticated management software as more formal properties open — but BMS's near-term market is the existing mid-tier and independent segment.

### Growth drivers

Uganda tourism growth is driven by gorilla trekking (Bwindi Impenetrable Forest), wildlife safaris (Queen Elizabeth, Murchison Falls), and the country's positioning as a high-value, low-volume destination. The government's 2025 target of 1.6 million arrivals reflects sustained investment in tourism infrastructure and marketing.

## 2. How Lodges and Hotels Currently Manage Operations

### The current technology reality

The technology stack at most Ugandan lodges sits in one of four tiers:

**Tier 1 — Paper and phone (majority of the market):**
Most smaller lodges and guesthouses track bookings in physical registers, manage stock with manual counts, communicate via WhatsApp group chats, and produce end-of-day cash reports on paper. Data loss during power cuts is a documented problem. Staff accountability is enforced informally.

**Tier 2 — WhatsApp + spreadsheets (very common):**
The upgrade from paper. Reservations confirmed on WhatsApp, tracked in a shared Google Sheet. Stock counted monthly. Reporting done by the GM manually at month-end. This is functional but opaque — nothing is connected, nothing is automatic, patterns are invisible.

**Tier 3 — Karibu or equivalent local PMS ($75–125/month):**
Hotels with 20+ rooms and a more formalised operation are the primary Karibu market. Karibu covers reservations, front desk, POS, inventory, and accounting — but as a set of modules that record what happened, not a system that analyses or connects the data.

**Tier 4 — International cloud PMS (rare in Uganda):**
A small number of properties (predominantly Kampala city hotels and premium safari lodges serving international guests) use Cloudbeds, Hotelogix, or similar. Usage is sparse — Cloudbeds has at least one Uganda property (Arcadia Lodges Bunyonyi), but no documented market strategy here.

### Where the pain is sharpest

Research and documented cases highlight five pain points that repeat across the Ugandan market:

1. **Staff fraud and revenue leakage.** The Rafiki Lodge case (Sipi Falls, documented in The Ankole Times) is illustrative: a former employee defrauded tourists of 60 million UGX using fake invoices, after being dismissed, because no digital records existed to prevent it. Cash dominance, paper receipts, and absence of linked stock-to-sales records create consistent opportunities for loss. This is not unique to Rafiki — it is endemic.

2. **Lack of actionable operational data.** A Kenyan study (Meru County, 2024 — African Journal of Tourism and Hospitality Management) found that 82.1% of hotels agreed ICT tools improved guest satisfaction and 78.8% reported better operational efficiency — but only 59.4% said technology improved their decision-making. The data is being recorded but not used.

3. **Double bookings and overbookings** from disconnected systems or manual channel management. Common at properties using WhatsApp for reservations while also appearing on Booking.com.

4. **Cash flow opacity.** Most guests pay cash. Tracking cash receipts, reconciling against stock consumption, and detecting discrepancies requires either good systems or a trusted accountant physically present. Most lodges have neither.

5. **Staff accountability without visibility.** Owners and GMs who cannot be on-site every day have no reliable mechanism to verify what is happening in their absence. This is the core problem BMS's intelligence layer is designed to solve.

## 3. The Technology Purchasing Decision

### Who decides

At a Ugandan lodge or hotel, the purchasing decision for management software typically sits with:
- **The owner (most commonly):** Especially at owner-operated properties. They feel the pain directly and have the authority to sign up.
- **The General Manager:** At properties where the owner is remote or has delegated operations. The GM experiences the daily friction and recommends solutions upward.
- **Finance Manager (less common):** May be the champion for accounting modules specifically.

### What they care about

Based on regional patterns and MTN Uganda's digitisation partnership research:
- **Price clarity.** Karibu's $75–125/month tiered pricing is understood and budgeted for. Any new system that obscures pricing or charges per-module will face friction.
- **Ease of adoption for non-technical staff.** The GM can decide to buy software, but if the chef, receptionist, and housekeeping supervisor cannot use it, the purchase fails. Staff training burden is a major adoption killer.
- **Offline reliability.** A system that stops working when the internet goes down is unusable at a rural lodge. This is not a nice-to-have — it is a deal-breaker.
- **Local support and trust.** Karibu's decade-long market presence provides a trust advantage. "They're based in Kampala" matters. A new product must establish trust rapidly, ideally through visible references (other lodges using it successfully).
- **Visible ROI.** Decision-makers want to see what the system saves them — reduced stock losses, better staff accountability, fewer double bookings — before they pay. A trial period or proven reference customer is important.

### What kills adoption

- Systems that are too complex for non-technical staff to operate daily
- Systems that require stable internet connectivity
- Systems where the initial configuration requires technical expertise or vendor involvement
- Systems with opaque pricing or unexpected costs

## 4. Economic Context

### What lodges pay today

Karibu's pricing is the market reference:
- 1–35 rooms: $75/month
- 36–75 rooms: $90/month
- Above 75 rooms: $125/month

This is the floor. A lodge currently on Karibu is already spending $75–125/month on management software. The question for BMS is not whether to price in this range, but whether to price above it (if BMS demonstrably delivers more value) or at it (to compete directly on switching costs).

Hotel360, a Kenya-based alternative, prices at approximately 1,500 KES/month (~$12/month at current rates) — a price point that signals a stripped-down, bookkeeping-focused product rather than a full management platform.

### Willingness to pay more

MTN Uganda's partnership with UHOA to digitise 780+ hotels signals that the sector recognises the need for technology investment. Reserveport (Pesapal's hospitality platform, backed by UHOA) launched in 2025 with multi-currency support and offline functionality — evidence that the market will adopt platforms that work in local conditions. (Source: SoftPower News, Tower Post News.)

Research indicates that willingness to pay above Karibu pricing is present if value is visible and demonstrable. A lodge losing 60 million UGX to fraud (the Rafiki case) would pay multiples of Karibu's monthly fee for a system that prevents it. However, this is not fully verified through direct market interviews — it is a reasonable inference from the documented pain.

### BMS pricing direction (inference, not decision)

A pricing range of $100–200/month for a fully featured BMS is plausible for Ugandan lodges with 20+ rooms, particularly if intelligence features (anomaly detection, accountability enforcement) are clearly visible. This is above Karibu but not dramatically so. For smaller operations or in competitive markets, a lower entry tier would reduce friction.

*This is not an architecture decision — it is a market inference for the architecture phase to consider.*

## 5. Infrastructure Realities

### Internet connectivity

Uganda's internet penetration is approximately 27–28% of the population as of early 2024, with 13.3 million users out of 49.25 million. Mobile connections are significantly higher at 67.7% (33.34 million active mobile connections). (Source: DataReportal Digital 2024: Uganda.)

The critical distinction: **mobile connectivity is not the same as internet connectivity.** A lodge in Murchison Falls or near the Rwanda border can have strong 3G/4G signal (sufficient for mobile money transactions and WhatsApp) but no reliable broadband. Rural areas face high last-mile costs and rely increasingly on satellite internet (Starlink has materially improved connectivity in some rural Uganda locations, though coverage is patchy). (Source: ITU Last-Mile Connectivity Assessment, Uganda 2024.)

**Practical implication for BMS:** The system cannot assume always-on internet. Data must be readable and writable locally. Sync must happen opportunistically when connectivity is present. This is not a niche edge case — it is the operating condition for many of BMS's target properties.

### Power reliability

Uganda's electricity grid suffers from load shedding, particularly in rural areas and during peak demand. Generator use is common at lodges. Power cuts create data loss risk for any system that relies on a continuous server connection. Cloud-hosted systems (like BMS) are insulated from on-premise hardware failure, but client devices losing power mid-form submission is a real failure mode.

**Practical implication:** Progressive save and auto-draft behaviour on all forms. No long-running forms that lose data if interrupted.

### Device availability

Most lodge staff interact with technology primarily through smartphones. Desktop computers are present at the front desk and the GM office but are not universal. A Ziwa chef, housekeeping supervisor, or security guard is far more likely to submit a daily report on their personal Android phone than on a desktop.

**Practical implication:** Mobile-first design is not optional. The HOD daily reports system proved this already — BMS inherits this learning.

### Mobile money dominance

MTN Mobile Money and Airtel Money are the primary payment infrastructure for most Ugandan consumers. Cash remains significant. Visa and Mastercard are used at premium properties serving international guests. Stripe is not a viable primary payment gateway in this market. (Source: MTN Uganda MoMo documentation, GovBill API documentation, general East Africa fintech research.)
